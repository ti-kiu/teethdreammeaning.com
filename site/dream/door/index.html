Dreaming of a Door: What It Means

The short reading

The two classical Arabic books do not give a door one price. An open door is a source of income — and the door of a house also represents the wife. A new door, or a carpenter setting one in place, is glad tidings of health and wealth. If you cannot close the door, both books read a difficulty coming from the wife; if you enter and lock it, they read protection. A small door inside the main entrance is a privacy breach, and may mean a secret affair; the received Chinese book (Zhou Gong) independently reads a door that opens by itself, or a small door opened in the house, the same way. A broken, fallen, or missing door is a blow to the household's guardian; fire on the door is priced in both Arabic books as the death of the wife, or a failure to manage the house, and Zhou Gong also treats fire on the door as ominous. The books are close to a mirror. They split on a few extra clauses — Al-Nabulsi prices a house with two doors as a corrupt wife; Zhou Gong prices a city gate standing wide open as a dispute, while the English compilation prices city gates as a righteous governor.

A door looks like a single symbol — open or shut, good or bad — but the classical entries are a ledger of switches: whether the door is open, new, broken, or on fire; whether a smaller door sits inside the main one; whether you can close it; whether it opens onto the street or into the house. The two Arabic books run nearly clause for clause; the received Chinese book (Zhou Gong) has a full chapter on doors and gates, and on several of those switches it agrees with them. Plain answers first — no Arabic or Chinese on this screen — every quote checked to its printed page.

An open or new door — income, or a turn for the better

In the compiled English dictionary attributed to Ibn Sirin, an open door represents a source of income, and the door of a house also represents the wife (printed p. 125). Al-Nabulsi's matching clause calls open doors the doors of sustenance (shamela 30, printed p. 33). A carpenter building a new door is glad tidings of health and wealth in the compilation; Al-Nabulsi reads a new door, or a carpenter setting one in place, as glad tidings of health. In Zhou Gong, a tall door, a newly opened door, a door that opens suddenly, or a wide-open door is generally auspicious, and renewing a door is read as the birth of a distinguished son. A door that opens by itself is a different switch, treated below.

A door that will not close — or that you lock behind you

If the dreamer cannot properly secure the closing of the door, the English compilation reads difficulties caused by the wife (printed p. 125). Al-Nabulsi's clause is the same difficulty, coming from the wife (shamela 30, printed p. 33). Entering the house and locking the door means protection against evil in the compilation, and protection from sin in Al-Nabulsi. Changing the door means moving to another house in the compilation; Al-Nabulsi reads a door pulled out and replaced with another as selling the house. Al-Nabulsi also prices opening a door as the easing of affairs, and blocking it as hardship and a cramped living. Zhou Gong reads a blocked door or gate as affairs that cannot proceed.

A small side door, or a door that opens by itself

A small door inside the main entrance means infringing on the privacy of someone else's bedroom, and may mean that one's wife has a secret affair or that a betrayal in the house will be uncovered (printed p. 125). Al-Nabulsi almost mirrors this: a small door in the middle of the house door is disliked, because it enters on private things, and betrayal will enter that house concerning the wife (shamela 30, printed p. 33). Zhou Gong independently reads a door that opens by itself as the wife having a secret affair, and opening a small door in the house as a secret affair. Al-Nabulsi has a further clause the English compilation does not: if the house has two doors, the wife is corrupt. Both Arabic books also read beasts or lions leaping at the door as insolent people pursuing the wife.

A broken, missing, or burning door

If the door is broken or burned, the English compilation reads difficulties for the resident guardian of the house; if the door disappears, it means death of the head of that household (printed p. 125). Al-Nabulsi reads a door that has fallen, been pulled out, burned, or been broken as a calamity for the guardian (shamela 30, printed p. 33). Fire burning the door is priced almost in the same words in both books: the death of the wife, or a failure to manage the household. Zhou Gong reads a broken door or gate as ominous, a ruined one as a great ill omen, and heavenly fire burning the door as an ominous matter. A door leaf that breaks by itself means servants leave; finding nobody inside the door or gate is a great ill omen.

The finding: one door, three ledgers

Set the compilation's "Door" entry at printed p. 125 beside Al-Nabulsi's door headword at shamela 30 (printed p. 33), and the clause order runs as a mirror: the door as the household's guardian; open doors as income or the doors of sustenance; house doors as women; a change in the door as a change in the owner's condition; a broken, fallen, or burned door as a blow to that guardian; a small door inside the main one as a breach of privacy and a possible affair; lions or beasts at the door as insolent people pursuing the wife; failing to find the door as indecision or worldly confusion; entering by the door as winning a dispute; a door that opens onto the street as benefit going to strangers; two rings or knockers as debt to two people; fire on the door as the death of the wife, or failed management of the house; a carpenter or a new door as news of health; being unable to close the door as a difficulty coming from the wife; changing or replacing the door as a move or a sale; and locking the door after entering as protection.

Two things are easy to misread, and both are on the page rather than in a later handbook. First, Al-Nabulsi adds a clause the English compilation does not: a house with two doors means the wife is corrupt. That is not the same as the two-knockers (two creditors) clause, which both books share, and it is worth keeping un-smoothed. Second, Zhou Gong has a full chapter on doors and gates — unlike the honest blank it left for the ladder — and on the household door it often agrees: open or new is generally good; a door that opens by itself, or a small door in the house, is a secret affair; a broken or fire-struck door is ominous. It splits with the Arabic books on the city gate: Zhou Gong prices a wide-open city gate as a dispute; the English compilation prices city gates as a righteous governor. This page does not stretch one into the other.

In one line

The books do not ask whether a door is a good omen. They ask whether it was open or new, whether you could close it, whether a smaller door sat inside the main one or the door opened by itself, and whether the door was broken, missing, or on fire — and they are closest to each other on the fire and on the small door.

Modern research

What modern research adds — and what it doesn't

No PubMed-indexed study that isolates door dreams as its own theme was found for this page. The closest checkable work is the Typical Dream Questionnaire of 55 themes, which does not include a door, a doorway, or a locked door as an item.

Nielsen, Zadra, Simard, Saucier, Stenstrom, Smith and Kuiken administered that questionnaire to 1,181 first-year university students in three Canadian cities (Dreaming 13(4):211–235, December 2003; 28.9% men, 71.1% women). The reprint of the paper was re-read today, including Appendix 1, which lists all 55 items, and Table 1, which gives lifetime prevalence by gender. There is no door item. The nearest items on that list are item 8, "being locked up," endorsed by 24.0% of the sample (men 22.9%, women 24.5%; rank 32), and item 53, "discovering a new room at home," endorsed by 32.3% (men 30.8%, women 33.0%; rank 21). Those are adjacent themes, not a door-dream rate, and this page does not treat them as one.

A related, PubMed-indexed typical-dream study is easier to check and does not fill the gap. Schredl, Ciric, Götz and Wittmann administered Zadra and Nielsen's 55-theme Typical Dream Questionnaire to 444 participants and named being chased, falling, flying, failing an examination, and being unable to find a toilet as examples; the rank order of themes was highly stable; the abstract, re-read today, does not name a door item, and the authors note that the question of the meaning of these themes, or their link to waking life, has not yet been answered.

So the modern layer can say that door dreams were not isolated as a typical theme in the standard 55-item list, and that "being locked up" and "discovering a new room at home" are measured, neighboring items. It cannot say how often people dream of a door as such, and it does not price an open door, a door that will not close, a small side door, or a door on fire.

This page reports what two classical Arabic texts, the received Chinese dream book, and two checkable modern papers can see about dreams of a door. It is not a prediction, a medical or religious ruling, and no dream book — classical or modern — settles what a dream means for the person who had it. If this dream unsettled you, note the move every source on this page makes in its own form: the books do not ask whether a door is lucky; they ask whether it was open or new, whether you could close it, whether a smaller door sat inside the main one, or the door opened by itself, and whether the door was broken, missing, or on fire — and they are closest to each other on the fire and on the small door.
