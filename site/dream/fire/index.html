Dreaming of Fire: What It Means

Both classical Arabic books open the fire entry with the same nine words, in the same order: glad tidings, a warning, war, chastisement, a ruler, imprisonment, losses, sins, or blessings (Ibn Sirin compilation, printed p. 158; Al-Nabulsi, shamela 348, printed p. 351 — five centuries apart, clause for clause). Before the ledger the compilation names two private readings — might, and love — and Al-Nabulsi keeps the love clause as its own line: a fire burning inside your heart is a love that overwhelms you. But no clause on this page is decided by the flame itself. The books flip the reading on four switches: day or night — fire seen in daylight means war and adversities, the same fire seen at night means peace and guidance, in both books; smoke — the more black smoke, the greater the danger; place — your house, the desert, the town, the sultan's door, the mountaintop each carry their own verdict; and harm — touched and unburned, someone keeps a promise to you; burning, the calamity falls on what the burning thing stands for. So the honest first answer is: these books do not read fire; they read when it burned, how much smoke it made, where it stood, and whether you were touched by it.

Fire belongs on this site for the same reason rain did: the classical books are clause machines, and the fire entries are the cleanest side-by-side test in either book — a two-page Arabic run and a three-page English entry that mirror each other nearly line for line, with a handful of collisions the mirrors leave unsmoothed. Plain answers first — no Arabic or Chinese on this screen — every quote checked to its printed page.

The nine-word ledger both books open with

The compilation's headword: fire signifies "glad tidings, a warning, war, chastisement, power, imprisonment, losses, sins, or blessings". Al-Nabulsi's Arabic, five centuries on, runs the same nine slots in the same order, the ninth word again blessing (quoted in full below). Then both books price the loud fire: sparks shooting every way, burning the wood, roaring with tumult — in the compilation, insubordination, civic disorder, an adversity through which many die; in Al-Nabulsi, a sedition through which people perish, "on the measure of what it burned" (printed p. 158; shamela 348, printed p. 351). The scale word — proportion — is in both.

Day fire, night fire — the first switch

The books agree in both directions. Seen by daylight, fire means war and adversities; seen at night, peace and guidance (printed p. 159 — in the scan the sentence prints with its words run together, kept as printed below). Al-Nabulsi's mirror clause closes the two-armies paragraph the same way: "it is said that fire seen in the day is a proof of war and sedition, and seen at night, a proof of comfort" (shamela 349, printed p. 352). Same switch, same two poles. Kindling has its own version: light a fire in the dark night so people can find the road, and you will acquire knowledge and guide people with it; light one on the bright road with no darkness needing it, and you are on the road of innovation, misleading others. Al-Nabulsi's Arabic again runs line-for-line with the English.

Two fires facing each other — and the water switch

Both books stage the same image: two burning thicket-loads trying to consume each other, two armies on the field. Whichever fire has more fuel is more numerous and harder-pressed; whichever the wind is with wins; whichever is blacker and darker has the worse intent; equal in color and harming nothing — two seditions sitting in that quarter. Then the switch that ties this page to the rain page: the army whose fire stands nearer the water is the one likelier to submit — and if the water overflows and quenches the fire, the water itself is debilitated and consumed, "and likewise if the rain comes down on it". Al-Nabulsi's mirror finishes the sentence the compilation started, page break and all (printed pp. 159-160; shamela 348-349, printed pp. 351-352). Fire quenched by rain is the rarest of the book's switches: in the compilation it means poverty or loss of your job; in Al-Nabulsi, your livelihood stops.

The harm switch — touched, unburned, eating

Fire that reaches you and does no harm means someone will fulfill a promise they made to you; Al-Nabulsi's same-page mirror: "whoever the fire touches and it does not burn him — his appointment will be honored" (printed p. 159; shamela 349, printed p. 352). Standing in the fire feeling cool and comfortable is the victory clause — truthfulness, sincerity, triumph over enemies in the compilation; in the Arabic, whoever sits in the fire and finds no heat attains truth, certainty, kingship. The burn switch flips: fire burning your garment or your skin means a calamity falling on you, or on the family member that limb represents. And eating fire? Swindling orphans' property or earning suspicious, unlawful money — both books, in the same words, quoting the same Qur'anic idiom; Al-Nabulsi adds the table setting: eating from gold or silver plates, and then poverty and begging.

Fire and the house — five verdicts

The books read five different house-fires. Fire falling into your house: in Al-Nabulsi, fertility and plenty; in the compilation, war and destruction if it rages, a new-disease spread if it makes no noise. Fire going out of your house: appointment, business, strength, trade-skill — governorship, trade, or craft-power in the Arabic, mirror for mirror. The house-light being extinguished: the death of the father, husband or child, and an extinguished fire in the town means the death of its governor, its great scholar, its renowned shaikh; in Al-Nabulsi, the manager of the house dies, the head of the town dies. A fire kindled on the mountaintop: devotion, needs met, safe return home — and in both books' next breath, a fire blazing in your own house ruins it. The two-armies clause and the torch clause (a burning torch with no smoke at your door: pilgrimage, house-renovation or marriage) sit between the extremes.

Smoke, and what smoke hides

Smoke is a gauge and a divider. The more black smoke a fire makes, the greater the danger and consequent sufferings (printed p. 159); Al-Nabulsi: the higher the smoke, the greater the terror. But fire with no smoke is its own species: it represents spirits or jinns — "for they are created from a smokeless fire" — and also drought, locusts, calamity. The Arabic entry keeps the same clause with the same theology and the same two plagues (printed p. 160; shamela 349, printed p. 352). A clean-flamed blaze is never simply cleaner: it changes what the fire is made of.

Fire in the mouth, the fingers, the palm — the teeth page's neighbors

Three short body clauses sit together in both books, and they are the ones a teeth-dream reader should notice. Fire in the fingers: an unjust person — or changing the contents of a book, or perjury. Fire lit in the palm: cutting corners in your trade, violating your own trade's code. Fire inside the mouth: distress — says the compilation, and so says Al-Nabulsi in one word of grief (quoted verbatim below), extending the ledger: fire in the fingers is the scribes' injustice, fire in the palm the artisan's. The mouth clause is this site's own entry point: the same books that read a falling tooth as a family ledger read a flame inside the mouth as grief with no omen in it (printed p. 160; shamela 349, printed p. 352).

Prices, sickness, possession

Fire burns goods, and the books read the market. A fire inside a granary means hiking prices; whatever product burns is in high demand and rising — and Al-Nabulsi's mirror: fire burning grain sends its price up, fire falling on any commodity makes it dear with many buyers. Fire in winter is the warm exception: warming yourself from a nearby fire on a cold day means becoming rich; in the Arabic, fire in winter means fruit — "they say, fire is winter's fruit". A fire talking to you out of a jar or pitcher means an evil spirit has possessed your son or daughter — the Arabic names it as jinn-speaking-on-the-tongue possession. And fire seen without flame and without roar? The compilation: the spread of a new disease; the Arabic: illnesses and events falling there.

The finding: one flame, four switches

Set the two entries side by side — Al-Nabulsi's (نار) run at shamela 348-350 (printed pp. 351-353) and the compilation's Fire entry at printed pp. 158-160 — and the clause order is a mirror almost head to head: the nine-word ledger, then the loud-sparks civic sedition with its proportion clause, then heart-fire as love, then the two fires as two armies with fuel, wind, blackness, water-nearness and the quenching water's own ruin, then the smoke gauge, then the night-kindled guide of knowledge and the daylight innovation, then the day/night war-versus-comfort switch, then fire-worship, then winter-warming wealth, then eating fire and orphans' property, then the condemned and the entered-and-exited hell, then the lighted fire ringed by a blessing group, then touch-without-burn as promise kept, then grain prices, then the empty pot, then the governor's smothering fire, then torch-at-door without smoke, then fingers, palm and mouth, then the plague list, then roasted meat and backbiting, then garment and limb, then borrowed fire as unlawful money, then the glare of tongues, then the sultan's door, then fire going out of the house, then east-to-west beams, then the falling-in fertility line, then the mountaintop, then the oven pregnancy, then sitting in the fire, then the sky-fire, then the extinguished fire and its deaths, then the vessel-fire and the jinns. Where the two books meet, they meet in order. On top of the mirror, this fire page shows four switches the teeth and rain pages also found working in these books: time (day war, night guidance; winter fire the one warmly positive case), smoke (a danger gauge upward, and a species marker at zero: no smoke means jinns, not safety), place (house, town, desert, sultan's door, mountaintop, granary, oven — five different house-fires alone), and harm (touched and unburned = a kept promise; burned = the calamity on what the thing represents; eaten = unlawful money).

And four collisions no summary repeats. Between the books: sky-fire that burns and does not affect you. The compilation reads the harm-free sky-fire as verbal abuse with no lasting consequences; Al-Nabulsi, in the same words about the same fire, reads it as soldiers descending on your house — same image, private insult versus public garrison. The extinguished house-light runs the other way: the compilation hangs death clauses on it (father, husband, child, town's scholar); Al-Nabulsi adds a burglary clause the English never keeps — a fire that goes out in your house and a wind comes in and lights it again: thieves enter. Inside the Arabic book: a citron — نارنج, the sour orange — means an evil or a sedition, and a house full of citrons means fire blazing in it; the book says the reading is taken "from its name, its color and its sourness" — the word contains نار, fire (shamela 360, printed p. 363). A whole verdict built on a pun the English can only report. Inside the alphabetic book: the fireplace entry ends by filing fire under the body and the table — the hearth represents your stomach, and its firewood a late heavy meal "that will cause indigestion or confused dreams" (printed p. 161): a 1992 compilation that tells you where confused dreams come from, printed on the page with the word run together. Across the languages: the Chinese book's fire verdicts sort neither by smoke nor harm but by direction and rank — fire burning the sun, moon, rivers, hills or heaven brings patrons, long life and national peace; standing in fire a noble helper; flame blazing wealth — while fire rising from the ground, black smoke, carrying fire down the road and putting fire to a well bring illness and blocked roads. Fifteen verdict lines, no ledger, no switch: the shapes do the work.

Modern research

What modern research adds — and what it doesn't

Modern dream research treats fire as a countable theme, not a sign. The numbers below were pulled and re-read today from the study itself. Johannes Mathes and Michael Schredl coded 1,612 dreams written down in diaries by 425 psychology students over two weeks, and scored each report against the 56-item Typical Dream Questionnaire: fire content (item 34) appeared in 1.74% of the diary reports; 6.60% of dreamers recorded at least one fire dream inside the fortnight; and 25.94% reported having dreamed of fire at least once in their lifetime. Fire ranked 17.5 among themes in the diaries and 30.5 on the lifetime questionnaire, with a small diary-questionnaire correlation (r = 0.094) — and the paper's own review sentence puts fire in the list of "archaic" themes (with earthquake, snakes, spiders and insects) that "occur relatively rarely" in adults (Mathes & Schredl, Somnologie 18:106–111, 2014, DOI 10.1007/s11818-013-0653-6; PDF re-pulled and table re-read today; student sample, stated as such). The same table is useful for this site's main subject too: teeth falling out (item 18) ran at 0.56% of diary reports — rarer in two weeks of actual diary life than fire, though 35.08% endorse it lifetime, a gap the authors themselves flag as unexplained. Questionnaire work adds only stability: across 444 participants the rank order of the 55 typical themes was stable, men reporting physical-aggression dreams more than women (Schredl, Ciric, Götz & Wittmann, The Journal of Psychology 138(6):485–494, 2004, PubMed 15612605, record re-pulled today). The 2018 teeth-dream paper this site leans on elsewhere (PubMed 30319507) has no fire item anywhere — its full text, re-checked today, contains no form of the word. Against the classical layer the direction is the story again: the Arabic books read fire as a civic sign — war, sedition, prices, governors, thieves, armies — while the modern instrument counts how many students wrote "fire" down in fourteen days, and finds the flame is rare. The gap is reported, not papered over.
