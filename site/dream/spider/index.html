Dreaming of a Spider: What It Means

Both classical Arabic books read a spider in a dream first as a person, not an omen. The Ibn Sirin compilation: a spider represents "a malicious woman, or a weak, perfidious and a distant man." Al-Nabulsi: "a cursed woman who deserts her husband's bed." Then the entries widen in the same breath: the spider also stands for a weaver, an ascetic or a monk; seeing one may mean meeting a pious and religious man; the compilation even allows "a pleasing wife." Weaving a web means becoming weak; a spider hanging from the ceiling means a severe winter in that area. And the oldest surviving Chinese dream record flips the verdict — the old Meng shu fragments read seeing a spider as joyous events on that day.

So the honest first answer is: the books never read the spider as one thing. The same insect carries a deceiver, a craftsman, a saint and — in one book — a pleasing wife, and the books list the alternatives without resolving them. What decides the reading is the scene: whose bed, whose ceiling, whether the spider weaves or you do. The scenarios people actually dream are below: plain answers first, no Arabic on this screen, every quote checked to its printed page.

You saw a spider — the general reading

Both entries open with a person. The compilation: "a spider represents a malicious woman, or a weak, perfidious and a distant man." Al-Nabulsi, same shape, harsher detail: a cursed woman who deserts her husband's bed; and whoever sees a spider "sees a scheming man," weak and irresolute. Then both books add the second and third faces: the spider is a weaver; it is an ascetic or a monk. The compilation: seeing a spider "may mean that he will meet a pious and a religious man." Al-Nabulsi: whoever comes across a spider "keeps the company of a devout ascetic." Deceiver, craftsman, saint — one insect, three candidates, and the entry leaves the choice with the details of your dream.

A spider web, or the spider weaving

The web is read as frailty, not as craft. The compilation, flatly: "Weaving a web in a dream means becoming weak." Al-Nabulsi grounds the same verdict with scripture: the spider's house and its weaving are weakness and frailty — and he cites the Quran directly: "the weakest of houses is indeed the spider's house" (29:41). Al-Nabulsi alone adds the trade clause: the spider can also mean knowledge of weaving and needlework. Follow his citation chain one step further and the web darkens: the compilation's own "Weaver" entry says seeing a weaver "may indicate the death of sick person, or lowering his corps into his grave" — a death reading attached to the spider's trade, but never stated in the spider's entry itself.

A spider hanging down from the ceiling

This is the clause the two books mirror almost word for word. The compilation: "If one sees a spider hanging down from the ceiling in a dream, it indicates a severe winter in that area." Al-Nabulsi: a spider seen falling from the ceilings is evidence of the severity of winter — same insect, same ceiling, same season, a thousand years apart. Notice what it does to the entry: a book that has spent the whole clause list reading the spider as a person suddenly reads it as a weather forecast for a whole district. Neither author edits the other face out.

A tarantula, or a spider that harms you

The compilation gives the dangerous spider its own entry, and it is the sternest person in the pair: a tarantula represents "a loathsome and an evil woman who interferes with people's business and damages their interests, or who destroys what they repair and denies having anything to do with their sufferings." The entry even explains where the reading comes from — the legendary nervous disease once believed to follow a tarantula's bite — and then adds that this harmless hairy spider, seen in a dream, "could represent a lethal enemy of a fierce sting, scowling, or a despising look." The bite-scene, in other words, is read as a face, not as venom.

What the Chinese tradition says

Stated plainly: the received Zhou Gong dream book — the 27-chapter text this site quotes everywhere else — carries no spider entry at all. We checked the corpus line by line; the word does not appear, and this page does not stretch its beetle or worm lines to fill the gap. Older Chinese material does survive, quoted inside the 10th-century encyclopedia Taiping Yulan: two lines from an earlier Meng shu dream book say that dreaming of a spider brings joyous events on that day — while a second line in the same quotation ties the spider to a worry about a pregnant woman. Same object, two verdicts, same book: exactly the structure the Arabic entries show. Folk lore kept the cheerful reading: the long-legged spider was called "little joy," and its arrival at a person announced a visiting relative.

The finding: two books mirroring, one entry arguing with itself

Set the two Arabic entries side by side and the clause order rhymes: cursed / malicious woman first, then the weak scheming man, then the weaver, then the ascetic or monk, and finally the ceiling clause and its severe winter. Al-Nabulsi (d. 1731) wrote a millennium after the material attributed to Ibn Sirin, and his Arabic and the compilation's English run the same machine — with each book holding one card the other does not: the compilation alone offers "a pleasing wife," and alone prices the bite through its separate Tarantula entry; Al-Nabulsi alone brands the spider "the sorceress woman," adds the weaving-trade clause, and quotes the Quran to prove the web is frailty. The deeper disagreement is inside each entry, not between them: the same paragraph that calls the spider a malicious woman calls it a pious man's counterpart a sentence later, and Al-Nabulsi's cursed woman who deserts her husband's bed shares one headword with the devout ascetic whose company you keep. These books are not deciding whether the spider is good or bad. They are saying the spider is a mask — and what wears it, woman, craftsman, monk or season, is the actual content of the dream.

And the Chinese record adds the reversal no folk summary of "spider dream meaning" repeats: the oldest quoted dream book on the spider — older than the received Zhou Gong text, preserved only because an imperial encyclopedia copied it — reads the insect as joy arriving that day, while the Arabic books read it as a deceiver. And even that old Chinese dream book carries two lines that disagree with each other — joy that day, and a worry about a pregnancy. The long-legged "little joy" announces a guest; Al-Nabulsi's spider deserts a marital bed. Same eight legs, opposite door.

What modern research adds — and what it doesn't

No typical-theme survey has published a prevalence figure for dreams of spiders: the standard questionnaires (the 55-item Typical Dreams Questionnaire and its relatives) ask about being chased, falling, and teeth — not about species. The closest measured record is a single-case diary analyzed by Schredl (International Journal of Dream Research 6(1):59–64, 2013, DOI 10.11588/ijodr.2013.1.9609): 8,420 dreams recorded by one man over 23 years — animals present in about 7% of the dreams (577 of them), spiders appearing 14 times, and spiders and snakes topping the table of species involved in negative interactions (the spider was the most named species among dreams of killing or hurting an animal, and among fighting). The paper is explicit that these negative spider and snake contacts had no counterpart in the dreamer's waking life — he kept cats and a hamster, not arachnids — and attributes them to phylogenetic prepared fears rather than waking-life contact. The fear side is well measured, at the group level: in the Swedish community sample behind that hypothesis (704 adults aged 18–70, randomly drawn; abstract re-pulled today from PubMed 8561762), animal phobia — the category Fredrikson and colleagues' factor analysis puts spiders and snakes in, with spiders being the field's standard exemplar — had a point prevalence of 12.1% in women and 3.3% in men (Fredrikson et al., Behaviour Research and Therapy 34(1):33–39, 1996; the spider-specific 3.5% — 5.6% of women, 1.2% of men — is how that paper is quoted in later review literature and is reported here as a secondary figure, not as text we re-read from the primary table). Specific phobia overall runs at 7.4% lifetime across 22 countries, with a median onset age of 8 (Wardenaar et al., Psychological Medicine 47:1744–1760, 2017, PubMed 28222820 — abstract re-read today). One further paper belongs on a spider page by sheer symmetry: jumping-spider spiderlings cycle through regular bouts of retinal movement and leg curling during night rest — an REM sleep-like state in an invertebrate (Rößler et al., PNAS 119(33):e2204754119, 2022, PubMed 35939710) — a finding about the spider's own sleep, not about anyone's dream of it. That is the honest line under this page's mirror: research counts how often the insect appears and how many people fear it; the classical books assign what the mask is worn by — and neither layer contains a variable called "what the dream means."

This page reports what two classical Arabic texts, the received Chinese dream book (including its honest silence on the spider), and adjacent published research can see about dreams of spiders. It is not a prediction, a medical or religious ruling, and no dream book — classical or modern — settles what a dream means for the person who had it. If this dream unsettled you, note the one move every source on this page makes in its own form: the spider is read as whatever is wearing it — a person, a trade, a season, a guest — not as the animal itself.
