Dreaming of a Ladder: What It Means

The short reading

The two classical Arabic books do not give a ladder one price. A wooden ladder means distress and toil during a journey — and, in the next sentence, a ladder also means peace and safety. Climbing a new ladder (or even an old one) can mean a rise in one's religion, trade, or station; climbing can also be a warning to do good, issued to someone who will not listen. Falling means adversities and a spiritual fall; climbing down means business losses; if the ladder breaks mid-way, one's opponent wins. A raised ladder means recovery from illness. The books disagree on only one switch: a ladder lying on the floor is authority in the English compilation and illness in Al-Nabulsi. Both also carry a darker clause — climbing a wooden ladder can mean seeking help from hypocrites, or eavesdropping. The received Chinese book (Zhou Gong) has no ladder entry.

A ladder looks like a single symbol — up or down, good or bad — but the classical entries are a ledger of switches: what the ladder is made of, whether it is new or old, whether it stands or lies flat, whether you climb, descend, fall, or watch it break. The two Arabic books run nearly clause for clause, five centuries apart; they split on one laying-down clause; and the Chinese book has no entry to set beside them. Plain answers first — no Arabic or Chinese on this screen — every quote checked to its printed page.

Climbing — a rise, and a warning

"Climbing a new ladder in a dream means reaching new spiritual attainments, or it could represent his business success. Even climbing an old ladder in a dream means success in one's bushiness, exaltation and profits" (printed p. 247 — the scan prints "bushiness" for "business," kept as printed). Al-Nabulsi's Arabic is the same split by age of the ladder: a new climb brings good and elevation in one's religion and world; an old climb brings good and elevation from trade or the like, and if one is in a dispute, one prevails (shamela 163, printed p. 166). That is only one face of the climb. The same English entry opens the act as "receiving a warning or an admonition to do good and eschew evil for someone who does not oblige." Al-Nabulsi reads climbing a wooden ladder as commanding good for someone who will not obey, or forbidding wrong for someone who will not stop. So the honest first answer is not "climbing means success." It is: the books price the climb twice, as a rise and as a warning issued to someone who will not take it.

Falling, or the ladder breaks

"Falling from a ladder in a dream means adversities, spiritual fall and temptation" (printed p. 247). Al-Nabulsi names the metal: a fall from an iron ladder means a pause or weakness in one's religion, and turning back from what one was upon (shamela 163, printed p. 166). A break is a different switch. "If the ladder breaks in the middle of one's assent or descent in the dream, it means losing to one's competition" (the scan prints "assent" for "ascent," kept as printed). Al-Nabulsi's clause is the same contest: if the ladder breaks while one is on it, one's opponent succeeds. A fall is your own drop; a break is a contest you lose. Popular "falling off a ladder" questions often fold the two together; the entries do not.

Climbing down, or the ladder lies flat

"Climbing down a ladder in a dream means business losses" (printed p. 247). Al-Nabulsi: descending an old ladder means a loss in one's trade, with no profit (shamela 163, printed p. 166). The exit hatch, in both books, is a descent onto something known: "Ifone fixes a ladder to descend upon something he knows in a dream, it means safety from danger, arrogance, fears, or escape from an accident that might cost him his life" (the scan joins "Ifone," kept as printed). Al-Nabulsi's matching clause is safety from delusion, fear, and destruction. A raised or standing ladder means recovering from an illness in both books. The one real split on this page: "A ladder laid on the floor in a dream means authority" in the English compilation — while Al-Nabulsi reads a ladder laid down as illness. Same posture, two prices.

Zhou Gong has no ladder entry

The received Chinese dream book this site quotes chapter by chapter has no ladder line. The 27-chapter corpus was searched for the ordinary character for a ladder and for the usual compounds for a staircase or for climbing a ladder; none occur. This page does not stretch the book's separate "ascending to heaven" lines into a ladder reading. The absence is stated here, the way this site states it when a symbol is missing, rather than filled in from a later popular edition.

The finding: one ladder, two ledgers

Set the compilation's "Ladder" entry at printed p. 247 beside Al-Nabulsi's climbing-ladder headword (the Arabic title is the "ladder of ascent") at shamela 163 (printed p. 166), and the clause order runs as a mirror: a wooden ladder as distress and toil on a journey; a ladder as peace and safety; climbing as an admonition for someone who will not listen; a wooden structure that becomes a solid building as firmness and a covering of what one hopes to hide; a ladder as a person of high rank, or as a sultan; new climb and old climb both priced as a rise; a fall; a descent as a loss in trade; a break as the opponent's win; a descent onto a known place as an escape from danger; and then the darker finish — a wooden ladder as a high-ranking hypocrite, climbing as seeking help from hypocrites, climbing in order to hear someone as attaining power, and the ladder as eavesdropping or carrying news from the good to the bad.

Two things are easy to misread, and both are on the page rather than in a later handbook. First, the English compilation and Al-Nabulsi disagree on the ladder that lies flat: authority versus illness. That is the only clause-level split in an otherwise tight mirror, and it is worth keeping un-smoothed. Second, Al-Nabulsi's same printed page carries two other headwords that share the Arabic spelling of "ladder" but are not this symbol: a greeting of peace, and a kind of advance sale. This page quotes only the climbing-ladder entry. The Chinese book contributes an honest blank, not a third ledger.

In one line

The books do not ask whether a ladder is a good omen. They ask whether you climbed a new one or an old one, whether you fell or the rungs broke, whether you climbed down onto something you knew, and whether the ladder was standing or lying flat — and they do not agree about the one that is lying flat.

Modern research

What modern research adds — and what it doesn't

There is a checkable study that actually names a ladder, but it does not give the ladder its own rate. Alfio Maggiolini, Alessandra Persico and Franca Crippa analysed 685 dreams from male and female subjects aged 10 to 32, and they registered gravity contents that include falling, flying, water, climbing, descending, staircases, and elevators — and, in the same list, "going up or down on a ladder or in an elevator." Gravity content of some kind appeared in 38.1% of the sampled dreams. The authors also compared those findings with a word search in DreamBank (N = 14,193). The paper is "Gravity Content in Dreams," Dreaming 17(2):87–97, 2007, DOI 10.1037/1053-0797.17.2.87. Those figures are from the published abstract, re-read today from the journal record and from the University of Milano-Bicocca open archive (which lists the citation but has no full-text file attached).

The same paper's category table, visible in a full-text preview of the article, defines the Stairs category as "Presence or use of ladders, stairs, elevators." That is the honest limit: ladder is bundled with stairs and elevators. This page does not invent a ladder-only percentage from a table it could not fully re-read. The article is not indexed in PubMed (a title search today returned no record). The abstract also says the results "tend to confirm a link between gravity content and sexuality"; without the correlation table in hand, that claim is noted, not restated as a finding of this page.

A related, PubMed-indexed typical-dream study is easier to check and does not fill the gap. Schredl, Ciric, Götz and Wittmann administered Zadra and Nielsen's 55-theme Typical Dream Questionnaire to 444 participants and named being chased, falling, flying, failing an examination, and being unable to find a toilet as examples of common themes (Journal of Psychology 138(6):485–494, 2004, PubMed 15612605, abstract re-read today). The abstract does not name a ladder item, and this page did not re-read the 55-item list in full today, so it does not claim the list has no ladder theme — only that the abstract, which is what was checked, does not mention one. A direct PubMed search for ladder or staircase dreams as a study topic did not turn up a dedicated paper.

So the modern layer can say that going up or down a ladder has been grouped with other gravity contents, and that falling and flying are well-measured typical themes. It cannot say how often people dream of a ladder as such, and it does not price a wooden ladder, a fall, a break, or a ladder lying flat. The classical ledger and the research ledger are not answering the same question.

This page reports what two classical Arabic texts, the received Chinese dream book (which has no ladder entry), and two checkable modern papers can see about dreams of a ladder. It is not a prediction, a medical or religious ruling, and no dream book — classical or modern — settles what a dream means for the person who had it. If this dream unsettled you, note the move every source on this page makes in its own form: the books do not ask whether a ladder is lucky; they ask whether you climbed a new one or an old one, whether you fell or the ladder broke, whether you climbed down onto something you knew, and whether the ladder was standing or lying flat — and they do not agree about the one that is lying flat.
