Dreaming of Rain: What It Means

Both classical Arabic books open the same entry with the same switch, in the same order: if no harm comes from the rain, it is blessings, provision and mercy — and if it destroys, the same water reads as punishment (Ibn Sirin compilation, printed p. 350; Al-Nabulsi, shamela 315, printed p. 318, a thousand years apart). Between those two poles the books install four switches folk summaries never carry: location — rain over your house alone is personal blessing and, in the next breath, an illness in that house; timing — rain in season blocks travel, trade and medicine, rain out of season is sickness; what the water was — stones or blood, dust or sand, honey or butter, swords or watermelons each get their own clause; and what you did — sheltering from the rain is slander coming your way, washing in it is repentance, drinking it is gain if it was clear and sickness if it was cloudy. So the honest first answer is: these books do not read rain; they read where it fell, when it fell, what came down with it, and whether you stood under a roof.

Rain belongs on a teeth-dream site by method, not by subject: this site's whole claim is that the classical books are clause machines, and no entry in them is a purer test than rain. One substance, one headword, and the valence flips on four coordinates. Plain answers first — no Arabic or Chinese on this screen — every quote checked to its printed page.

Rain fell and nothing was harmed — the general reading

The headword clause, in both books: good, provision, mercy. Al-Nabulsi then keeps two quiet life-clauses the English round-ups drop: rain may stand for the life of someone whose life is feared for, and for the fulfillment of what a person has been promised. The compilation's parallel chain runs life, an earthly being, or fulfillment of a promise — the same three columns. And in the same entry both books state a rare equation, reversible on its face: rain represents a caravan of camels, and a caravan of camels represents rain. Two images, one ledger line, in either direction.

The rain fell only on your house

Location is the sharpest switch, and the books price it twice. Rain falling exclusively on one known place means sorrow and distress for its dwellers — or the loss of a beloved, the compilation adds. But rain falling exclusively over the dreamer's own house is read as personal blessings — and, in the next sentence, as someone in that house falling sick or suffering debilitating pain. Al-Nabulsi mirrors both: private rain to your house means benefit, good and dignity, and if it covers the whole town the ruling extends to everyone in it. Same image, two columns — blessing and illness — because the house is a household, not a weather station.

The storm damaged things — the destruction clause

Cross the harm switch and everything flips. A destructive rainstorm, in the compilation, denotes dishonesty, cheating with measures, and stands for punishment on the corruption of that place's dwellers. Al-Nabulsi's mirror adds the civic register: the destroying flood is sedition and ruin falling on that place from the ruler, and rain that melts in every direction and cuts down trees is sedition from the sultan's side — and in a private house the same water becomes diseases, afflictions and smallpox. Stones or blood from the sky mean calamity and punishment for sins; dust or sand mean an unjust ruler in that locality; dirt without dust, oddly, means prosperity and a good harvest. The books even price the odd rain-falls: swords from the sky mean people are tested with arguing and litigation — and if the sky rains watermelons, they fall sick.

You sheltered from the rain — and the season switch

The shelter clause is one of the most quoted by dreamers and one of the least quoted by summaries: standing under a cover, a roof or behind a wall to escape the rain means suffering because someone slanders you — and Al-Nabulsi adds a penalty scale, that the shelterer may be struck in proportion to the rain that reached him. Then the timing switch: rain in season is read negatively — a roadblock barring travel, business losses, medicine a poor person cannot buy, or imprisonment — while Al-Nabulsi's tail clause prices the other pole: rain out of its time, in its hour of harm, means illnesses and ailments. On time it stops you; off time it sickens you. Neither book lets seasonal rain simply be good.

You washed in the rain, or drank it

The action clauses run positive. Washing yourself in the rain, performing ablution with it, washing your face or filth away: repentance from sin, spiritual guidance, and the abolishing of innovations from the heart — and Al-Nabulsi prices the same clause by person: a disbeliever who washes becomes Muslim, an innovator or sinner repents, the poor are enriched, and a request to a ruler is answered favorably. Drinking rainwater splits on clarity: clear and pure means blessings and benefits; dirty and polluted means a sickness, in proportion to what you drank. One water, three readings — mouth, skin, roof.

A general rain, and the death clause

Two clauses tie rain to this site's other pages. Al-Nabulsi keeps a general-rain clause: a rain falling on everyone revives for the dreamer a dead matter, and brings good, blessing and relief for the worried or indebted. And both books run the same stranger line in the death entry: the death of an unknown woman means the rain being withheld from the earth, and if she comes back to life, God revives the earth with rain — the rain page and the resurrection page quote the same clause from opposite sides.

Rain indoors — gutters and the Rain-Gutter of Mercy

People search this scene as "raining inside the house," and the classical answer is the plumbing, not the cloud. Al-Nabulsi's gutter headword, a page and a half after the rain entry: a gutter is a man of kindness — and the clause runs on to say that gutters running in no rain (the sentence breaks mid-word at the page edge here, and resumes on the next) indicate strife, each gutter a reckoning of the neck; running water clear while people benefit means prosperity and safety, murky or foul-smelling means disease; and whoever sees himself under the Rain-Gutter of Mercy — the Kaaba's own gutter — is met by mercy and receives what he hopes, exactly as the book ruled the well of Zamzam. The roof clause flips again: under a roof from rain is slander; under the gutter of mercy is answered prayer. Same geometry, opposite verdicts.

The finding: one cloud, four switches

Set the two entries side by side — Al-Nabulsi's (مطر) headword at shamela 315-316 (printed pp. 318-319), the compilation's Rain entry at printed p. 350 — and the clause order is a mirror: harm-free first (good, provision, mercy), then life, then the promised-thing fulfilled, then the location split (exclusive place = sorrow for its dwellers; own house = personal blessing and its illness double; whole town = everyone), then stones and blood, then dust and sand as the unjust ruler, then dirt-without-dust as harvest, then the traveller's hindrance, then the destruction clause and its measure-cheating, then reconciliation with enemies, then the camel caravan's two-way equation, then wells and springs, then reviving an old stagnant matter, then debts paid and relief felt, then the farmer's harvest, then honey butter oil and food rained down, then mercy, knowledge, wisdom, the Qur'an, rejuvenation, resurrection and life — and then the shelter, the season, the washing, the drinking. The inheritance is exact, entry for entry, in the same sequence. On top of the mirror, this rain page shows four switches the teeth pages also found in these books, working the same way: location (house versus town versus the known place), timing (in season it blocks; out of season it sickens), substance (the water's cargo: stones, blood, dust, dirt, honey, butter, oil, swords, watermelons), and action (shelter = slander, wash = repentance, drink = profit or illness by clarity).

And three collisions no English round-up repeats. Inside the books: rain over your own house is blessing and illness in one breath — the two columns of one clause, the same double-entry the teeth ledger keeps where one caught tooth cuts the line of heirs and another keeps the property. Inside the Chinese book: the received Zhou Gong text carries rain in exactly four verdict lines, all four in chapter 1 (the Heaven-and-Earth chapter), and they disagree by shape — clearing after rain takes a hundred worries away, grey rainy weather prescribes confinement, rain met on the road means food and drink, wild wind with heavy rain means a person's death — while that same chapter's lines make wind and rain co-sign the only fatal verdict. The Chinese book has no rain headword chapter; it has weather shapes. Inside the alphabetic book: Al-Nabulsi files rain next to its own near-spelling traps — a few columns on, مطرقة (mallet) is the police chief and whoever takes one gets abundant favor, and مطران (a metropolitan bishop) is a man of authority calling people to innovation — three headwords sharing the first three letters, a reminder that the classical entry you reach depends on the word you looked up. And the compiled English edition carries its own anachronism inside the clouds entry this page quotes: the translation reads that clouds in a dream "also represent ships, airplanes" — 1992 hands around an eight-century list.

The entries, briefly

The death-and-rain clause is shared: the compilation's "Death" entry at printed p. 111 and Al-Nabulsi's (موت) entry at shamela 344 (printed p. 347). The Chinese side is four verdict lines, all from chapter 1 of the received Zhou Gong text. One sample of each book, translated, then the full checked passages in the sources section at the bottom.

Modern research

What modern research adds — and what it doesn't

Modern dream research has never surveyed rain dreams in a population — we searched the published record today and there is no general sample, no questionnaire item, no prevalence number. Everything measurable comes from one diary. Michael Schredl analyzed a single dreamer's long series — 11,777 dreams recorded over 30 years — and found rain in about 1.40% of reports; the rain affected the dreamer in two-thirds of the rain dreams, usually with no emotion explicitly mentioned, and when emotions were named, negative ones outnumbered positive 48 dreams to 4 ("It's raining again" — Bad weather in a long dream series, International Journal of Dream Research 13(2):302–305, 2020, DOI 10.11588/ijodr.2020.2.75162; abstract re-pulled from the journal today). The same series, re-read in a companion report, showed the share of rain dreams rising across the decades (Spearman r = .435, p = .014) while cold elements — snow, ice, hail — fell, in step with Germany's shrinking count of ice days (Schredl, IJoDR 14(1):144–146, 2021, DOI 10.11588/ijodr.2021.1.78020, full text re-read today; single dreamer, stated as such, correlation of a diary share with climate indices — not a causal design). Older survey work supports only the general rule: season content shows continuity in winter but not in summer (Schredl, Perceptual and Motor Skills 98:1438–1440, 2004, N = 444, PubMed 15291235, record re-pulled today), and weather-adjacent measurement at population scale exists only for temperature, not rain: +1°C warmer nights produced about three extra nights of self-reported insufficient sleep per hundred people per month across 765,000 U.S. responses (Obradovich et al., Science Advances 3(5):e1601555, 2017, PubMed 28560320, record re-pulled today). The 2018 teeth-dream paper this site leans on elsewhere (PubMed 30319507) has no rain item anywhere in it — its full text, re-checked today, contains the letters "rain" only inside the journal abbreviation "Behav. Brain Sci." Set against the classical layer, the direction is the story: the Arabic books read rain as a public sign — for the town, the ruler, the harvest, the caravan — while the only modern instrument is one private dreamer's notebook, and its finding is that rain in his dreams mostly made him feel worse, in agreement with the books' destruction clauses and in silence on their mercy clauses. The gap is reported, not papered over.

This page reports what two classical Arabic texts, the received Chinese dream book (whose four rain lines disagree by weather shape), and one single-dreamer diary can see about dreams of rain. It is not a prediction, a medical or religious ruling, and no dream book — classical or modern — settles what a dream means for the person who had it. If this dream unsettled you, note the move every source on this page makes in its own form: the books do not ask whether rain is good or bad; they ask where it fell, when it fell, what came down with it, and what you did in it. For the same water read through a different door — drinking, drowning, clear and dirty — see water dreams, and for the clause where withheld rain and a dead woman are the same news, a dead person alive again.
