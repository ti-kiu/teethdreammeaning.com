Classical dream interpretation, read from the source books — not from other websites.

Start here: teeth falling out

Woke up from a teeth falling out dream? The plainest reading: something in your life feels like it's slipping out of control — money going out faster than it should, a family member you're quietly worried about, or a situation you're trying to hold onto that won't hold. The classical books land on that reading again and again. Read the full answer →

One tooth or the whole mouth?
The counting rules: one tooth pays one creditor, the whole mouth ends a household — and one page of Al-Nabulsi rules against itself on what catching the teeth means.

Bad omen or good sign?
Both verdicts are in the books, on the same pages: longevity, dirhams by count, a slave's freedom — death only when the tooth is lost or buried.

Is it caused by stress?
The classical books never call it a stress dream, and the study that measured distress found r = 0.02 — no link — while finding one to jaw tension on waking.

Other symbols

New pages as their sources are verified — one symbol at a time, three traditions where available.

Snake
The bite: Arabic books read disgrace, the Chinese book reads great wealth. Killing it is "marriage" on one line and "a child's death" on another.
Ibn Sirin p. 398 · Al-Nabulsi shamela 54 + 94 · Zhou Gong ch. 25
Water
Clear water = life, money, marriage. Water closing over you = trouble you're inside. Plus the thirst experiments that put water in dreams without symbolism.
Ibn Sirin p. 470 · Al-Nabulsi shamela 316 · Zhou Gong ch. 23
A dead person coming back to life
Both Arabic books read the revived dead as recovery — wealth after poverty, repentance, the traveler home — never a haunting. Zhou Gong: news arrives. The research layer: 58% of the bereaved have the dream.
Ibn Sirin pp. 111–114 · Al-Nabulsi shamela 344 + 345 · Zhou Gong ch. 18
The Kaaba
Seeing it means glad tidings and safety from fear; praying inside it means protection from your enemy — but praying on its roof means losing your faith. The tawaf clause grades your religion by the rites you actually did.
Ibn Sirin pp. 237–238 · Al-Nabulsi shamela 289 · Zhou Gong: no such entry (stated)
A wedding or marriage
Both Arabic books open the same way — God's care, then debt and office — and both read death as marriage, washed and perfumed like a bridegroom. Musicians at your wedding mean death in one book and a promotion in the other.
Ibn Sirin pp. 266–267, 473, 111 · Al-Nabulsi shamela 145 + 344 · Zhou Gong ch. 14: marriage = inauspicious
Gold
Both books open cold — gold is a disliked element that costs you property — then crown it: a necklace means leadership, spun gold means unbroken provision, and gold turning to silver means your fortunes tipping from increase to decrease.
Ibn Sirin p. 189 · Al-Nabulsi shamela 124 + 125 · Zhou Gong ch. 9: gold and silver treasures = wealth and honor
Hair falling out
Both Arabic books: hair means money and longevity, so shedding is a loss of savings, not a death sentence — and shaving means your debts get paid. The Chinese book flips two entries: white hair and regrowth read as long life.
Ibn Sirin p. 198 · Al-Nabulsi shamela 192 + 193 · Zhou Gong ch. 3: baldness and hair fall = inauspicious

The method, stated plainly

Every page on this site places the two classical dream books' original entries next to each other, each with a checkable location: the English compilation Ibn Seerin's Dictionary of Dreams (Pearl Publishing House, 1992 — a later compilation attributed to Ibn Sirin, not his autograph text), and the Arabic original of Al-Nabulsi's Ta'tir al-Anam (Dar al-Fikr edition), cited to the page in the online library where it can be read directly. Where a Chinese tradition source exists — the Zhou Gong dream book — its lines are quoted from the received text with chapter locations.

Where the books disagree, we show the disagreement instead of smoothing it over. Where one book contradicts itself, we show that too.
