Disclaimer

This site reports what classical dream-interpretation texts say. That is all it does. Read this page before relying on anything here.

What this site is

Each symbol page places two classical Arabic dream books side by side — the English compilation published as Ibn Seerin's Dictionary of Dreams (Muhammad M. Al-Akili, Pearl Publishing House, 1992) and the Arabic original of Al-Nabulsi's Ta'tir al-Anam — and, where one exists, a line from the Chinese tradition attributed to Zhou Gong. Every quotation is checked against a citable page: the printed page number of the compilation, the library page of the Shamela edition for Al-Nabulsi, and the chapter of the received Zhou Gong text. Where the sources disagree, the disagreement is shown, not smoothed over. Where a source is silent, the silence is stated.

What this site is not

  • It is not a prediction. No page on this site claims that a dream foretells an event. The classical books themselves frame interpretations as readings of symbols, not forecasts, and this site does not go beyond them.
  • It is not medical or psychological advice. Dream content can reflect stress, grief, sleep disorders, or medication effects. If recurring dreams disturb your sleep or mood, consult a qualified health professional. The research summaries on this site describe published studies; they do not diagnose anything about you.
  • It is not a religious ruling. The two Arabic books belong to the Islamic tradition of dream interpretation (ta'bir), and the compilation is a later work attributed to Ibn Sirin, not his autograph text. Nothing here is a fatwa, a spiritual judgment, or guidance on what God intends for you. For religious questions, ask a scholar you trust.
  • It is not financial guidance. Pages that discuss gold, money, or wealth report what the books say those symbols meant. They say nothing about what you should buy, sell, or invest.

About the sources

The compilation attributed to Ibn Sirin is a modern English work assembling material traditionally credited to the 8th-century scholar Abu Bakr Muhammad ibn Sirin, who lived two centuries before the earliest surviving dream manuals bearing his name. Al-Nabulsi (d. 1731 CE) wrote a millennium after Ibn Sirin. This site marks that distance on every page rather than hiding it — and where the two books agree clause for clause, that agreement is shown as a fact about the texts, not as proof of the interpretations' truth.

The honest bottom line

No dream book — classical or modern — settles what a dream means for the person who had it. What these books offer is a record of how dreams were read, clause by clause, with checkable locations. If a dream on this site unsettled you, the most useful sentence in the whole tradition may be the one both Arabic books repeat in different forms: the symbol stands for a change, not a sentence. Read, compare, check the page numbers — and take what the books say as history and literature, not as your future.
